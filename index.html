01 · Foundations

The nature of light

Light is an electromagnetic wave. The human eye only perceives a tiny slice of the full electromagnetic spectrum — the visible spectrum — and dispersion is precisely the phenomenon that reveals all the colours hidden inside "white" light.

🌊

An electromagnetic wave

Light is a coupled oscillation of electric and magnetic fields that propagates without a medium. Each wave is characterised by its wavelength λ and frequency f, linked by c = λ·f.

⚛️

Wave–particle duality

Light also behaves as a stream of photons. The energy of each photon is E = h·f, so violet photons (high f) carry more energy than red photons — a fact that matters in absorption and heating.

⚡

A universal speed limit

In vacuum, light travels at c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s. Inside matter it slows down — and it slows down differently for each colour. That single fact is the origin of dispersion.

02 · The mechanism

Refraction: light changes speed

When light crosses the boundary between two transparent media, its speed changes and the ray bends. The amount of bending is governed by Snell's Law. This is why a straw looks broken in a glass of water and a pool looks shallower than it really is.

The refractive index of a medium is n = c / v — the ratio between the speed of light in vacuum and in the medium. Air: n ≈ 1.0003 · Water: n ≈ 1.33 · Crown glass: n ≈ 1.52 · Diamond: n ≈ 2.42.

n₁ · sin θ₁ = n₂ · sin θ₂

Entering a denser medium (higher n), light slows down and the ray bends toward the normal. The larger the difference between the indices, the stronger the bend. Try the simulator: drag the angle and swap the medium — watch θ₂ shrink as n grows.

03 · The phenomenon

Dispersion: n depends on wavelength

Crucially, the refractive index is not the same for every colour. Light oscillates the electrons of the material as it passes; shorter wavelengths (violet, blue) interact more strongly and are slowed more than longer ones (red). Approximated by Cauchy's equation, n(λ) = A + B/λ², this means violet bends more than red — and white light fans out into a spectrum.

The differences look tiny (≈ 0.01 in glass), but after two refractions inside a prism they are enough to open the beam into a full rainbow. Diamond's huge n and strong dispersion (Δn ≈ 0.05) create its famous coloured flashes — the "fire".

Newton's experimentum crucis

1666

In a dark room, Newton passes a sunbeam through a glass prism and obtains an elongated spectrum — not a round spot.

1672

With a second, inverted prism he recombines the colours into white light: the colours were inside the light, not created by the glass.

1704

"Opticks" is published, establishing that white light is a mixture of all the spectral colours.

Before Newton, scholars believed the prism stained the light. His two‑prism experiment proved the opposite: refraction merely separates what is already there.

05 · In the wild

Dispersion around us

🌈

Rainbows

Each raindrop acts as a tiny prism: light refracts on entry, disperses, reflects on the back surface and refracts again on exit. Red leaves at ≈42°, violet at ≈40° — that's why red is always on top.

💎

Diamond "fire"

Diamond combines a huge refractive index with strong dispersion. Multiple total internal reflections spread the colours before they exit, producing the prized coloured flashes.

📷

Chromatic aberration

Camera and telescope lenses focus blue light slightly closer than red, creating colour fringes. Engineers cancel it by combining glasses with different dispersion (achromatic doublets).

🌇

Sunsets & the sky

Atmospheric refraction bends and spreads sunlight near the horizon; combined with scattering, it paints sunsets red and orange while the zenith stays blue.

💦

Spray & fountains

Mist from a garden hose or a fountain shows miniature rainbows on sunny days — dispersion on demand, at arm's length.

🔆

Crystal & glassware

Cut glass and crystal chandeliers throw coloured spots on walls and tables: every facet is a small refracting, dispersing surface.

06 · Daily life connection

From the spectrum to pool warming

The same physics of dispersion — sunlight as a mixture of wavelengths, each carrying energy, and refraction at material boundaries — is the operating principle of solar thermal collectors used to heat swimming pools. Understanding the spectrum tells us exactly why these panels are black and why a transparent cover boosts their performance.

  1. Sunlight arrives as a full spectrum

    Dispersion teaches us that white sunlight is a mixture of wavelengths (plus infrared). Every nanometre that strikes the collector delivers energy E = h·f to the material.

  2. Refraction at the transparent cover

    In glazed collectors, light refracts entering the glass/polycarbonate cover (Snell's law). The cover is chosen to transmit the visible spectrum almost completely while blocking the infrared re‑emitted by the hot plate — the greenhouse effect.

  3. A black absorber captures every colour

    A black surface absorbs all visible wavelengths — it is the exact opposite of a prism's job. No colour is reflected, so nearly the whole spectrum becomes thermal energy. That is why solar panels and collector pipes are black.

  4. Water carries the heat home

    Pool water pumped through the warm absorber pipes returns 1–3 °C hotter per pass, raising the pool temperature degree by degree — free energy, straight from the spectrum.

Bonus link: refraction is also why your pool looks shallower than it is — light from the bottom bends away from the normal on leaving the water, lifting the apparent depth. Dispersion and refraction are literally swimming with you.
